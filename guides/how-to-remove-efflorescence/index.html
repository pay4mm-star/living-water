How to Remove Efflorescence from Brick, Stone & Interlock

Short answer: Efflorescence is a white, powdery salt deposit left when water moves through brick, concrete, stone or mortar and evaporates at the surface. Light, fresh efflorescence can be dry-brushed off; established deposits need a masonry cleaner suited to the material, applied to a pre-wetted surface and thoroughly rinsed. It stops returning only when the moisture source is controlled or the material finishes curing.

What causes efflorescence?

Three things must be present: soluble salts in or behind the masonry, water to dissolve them, and a path for that water to reach the surface and evaporate. New brick, mortar, precast and concrete pavers contain more free salts, which is why new homes and new interlock often "bloom" in their first year or two.

Efflorescence vs other white stains

  • Efflorescence — powdery or crystalline, often brushes off when dry, reappears after rain.
  • Lime run / calcium carbonate — hard white crust, often streaking from mortar joints; needs stronger treatment.
  • Mortar smears — left from construction; removed with a properly neutralised masonry cleaner.

How professionals remove efflorescence

  1. Identify the material and the deposit — limestone, marble and some precast are acid-sensitive.
  2. Test a small area to confirm the cleaner will not discolour the surface.
  3. Pre-wet the surface so the cleaner stays on the face and is not drawn deep into the material.
  4. Apply the correct cleaner and agitate where needed.
  5. Rinse thoroughly with controlled pressure, and neutralise if an acid-based product was used.
  6. Address the moisture — downspouts, grading, failed caulking or missing caps.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Using undiluted muriatic acid — it can burn brick, etch stone and leave new stains.
  • Blasting with high pressure — it drives more water in and can damage the face.
  • Sealing over efflorescence — it traps the salts and can cause spalling.

Will it come back?

On new construction, efflorescence often returns a few times as the material cures, then stops. On older buildings, recurring efflorescence points to a moisture problem that should be fixed. Once the surface is clean and dry, a breathable sealer can help on pavers and some stone.

Does vinegar remove efflorescence?

Diluted vinegar can lift light efflorescence on durable brick, but it is acidic and can etch limestone, marble and some precast. Test first or use a product made for the specific material.

Is efflorescence harmful?

Efflorescence itself is cosmetic, but it shows water is moving through the masonry, which can lead to freeze-thaw damage over time.

Is efflorescence covered under paver or stone warranties?

Many manufacturers treat it as a natural occurrence; some use specialist contractors such as Living Water for warranty service. Check with your supplier.
